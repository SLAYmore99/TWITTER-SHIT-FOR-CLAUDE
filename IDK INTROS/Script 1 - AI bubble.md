# Script 1 - AI bubble

Topic: "Will the AI bubble pop?" Told through the dot-com crash and earlier bubbles. Story-first script by Claude. It holds no edit instructions on purpose: Rachit adapts to it.

## Current state
- Status: **draft v1**, about 90 words (roughly 40 s read aloud with pauses). Waiting for Rachit's challenge.
- The idea: the people in every bubble were right about the technology and still lost, because they were wrong about the price. The video that follows would start at the first bubble.
- Next step: Rachit reads it, says what he would change, then writes his own breakdown using the training wheels below as a starting point (see [[Intro workflow]]).

## Script v1
March 10th, 2000. The Nasdaq closes above five thousand.

Two and a half years later, it's down nearly eighty percent.

Here's the part nobody likes to admit: the people buying were right. The internet really did change everything.

Right about the future. Wrong about the price.

It happened with the railways in the 1840s. It happened with radio in the 1920s.

Now the biggest companies on earth are spending hundreds of billions of dollars on AI.

Is this a bubble? And if it pops... what's left standing?

To find out, we have to go back to the first time this happened.

## Training wheels (script 1 only, at Rachit's request)
Rough ideas with a reason, not a plan. Everything under "Your call" is Rachit's to decide. Remove these once the breakdown is his.

| Line | Music (rough) | Assets (rough) | Edit and type (rough) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. "March 10th, 2000... above five thousand" | Start nearly bare, then a low swell under the end of the line. A date and a number land best in space. | One image that says "the peak", not a generic bull. Cool, slightly grainy: the history look. | First cut waits for the swell. Hold the number a beat. Text: just the date or the figure, big. |
| 2. "down nearly eighty percent" | The swell collapses and the bass goes out on "down". The sound falls with the number. | Show the fall without the red-arrow cliche. Contrast with line 1: up against down, bright against dark. | A few fast cuts for time passing, then a hold on the drop. |
| 3. "...the people buying were right..." | My lean: the biggest silence of the intro, just before "were right", then the music returns warmer. The line reverses what the viewer expects. | Icons of the internet era in a quick burst, like Ref 1's "renaissance" burst: each readable in under 0.3 s, each a different kind of image. | Hold before the burst, burst on "changed everything". |
| 4. "Right about the future. Wrong about the price." | No new move. Thin the music and let the line stand. | Probably no new footage: the type is the asset. Two parts in contrasting styles. | A hold between "future." and "Wrong". |
| 5. "railways... radio..." | Same piece, maybe a riser creeping in to hand over to "Now". | One image per bubble, same shot size, so the repetition shows. History look. | Two matching beats. |
| 6. "Now the biggest companies..." | A new piece. Past to present changes both context and tempo. Riser + hit, or hard stop: your pick. | The look flips: saturated screens, scale, buildings, money. Avoid the glowing robot or brain. | The new look should feel like a different world within a second. |
| 7. "Is this a bubble?... what's left standing?" | The pulses stop and one thin note is held. The question needs room. | One slow, big image for each half of the question. | The longest holds of the intro go here. The holds are what make the bursts hit. |
| 8. "...go back to the first time" | Something that can carry into the main video's first scene. | A rewind feeling, or a shot that can match-cut into the first bubble. | A match cut on shape or movement. |

My lean on structure: 3 pieces of music. Lines 1-5 are one stretch (history, about the length of Ref 1's history stretch), line 6 is the new piece, lines 7-8 can be a texture stop and a handoff. It is a lean, not a rule.

Cliche traps to dodge: red falling arrows, the Wall Street bull, neon charts over bokeh, glowing robots and brains.
